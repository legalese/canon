# National Registration Act 1965 — naive encoding

**Method: naive.** Straight from the deposited text with the `writing-l4-rules`
skill and nothing else. No pipeline, no coverage table, no independent test
pass, no human gate.

**Edition:** 2020 Revised Edition, informal consolidation, incorporating Act 16
of 2026 (the cross-border railway amendments, in force 30 September 2026). The
deposit's arrangement of sections runs one ahead of the body; the encoding
follows the body.

**Checks:** two case files, 243 assertions satisfied, 0 errors, 0 warnings.

## Why this Act

**37 of the 527 Singapore Acts** deposited here name it, and **21 of those cite
s 10**, the change-of-address report: they deem a person to have told the agency
of a move if the person reports it under s 10. It is also the Act behind the NRIC
that every resident carries.

## Scope

The whole Act, except the appointment and delegation machinery, the register
itself (s 5), the cross-border railway jurisdiction (s 16A), the procedure for
arrested persons, bail and investigation (ss 20, 23, 23A, 24), and the
regulation-making power. **The National Registration Regulations were not
retrieved.** The exemptions from registration, the card categories, the
compoundable offences and any lawful authority to hand a card over all live
there.

## What the Act turns out to say

### 1. A late change-of-address report carries five years

s 10(1): report a change of residence "within 28 days". s 16(1)(b): failing to is
an offence punishable by **$5,000 or five years**, the same maximum as defacing or
destroying an identity card (s 16(1)(h)) and failing to register at all. Asserted
as that equality, and at 28 and 29 days.

### 2. Handing over your own NRIC has no reasonable-excuse defence

s 16(2)(a): a person who "**without lawful authority**, parts with possession of
any identity card to any person" commits an offence punishable by **$10,000 or ten
years**. s 16(1)(e) and s 16(2)(b) say "without lawful authority **or reasonable
excuse**". (2)(a) does not. Handing your card to a condominium guard, a hotel
receptionist or a phone shop is an offence unless some law authorises it, and a
reasonable excuse is no answer. s 33(2)(f) lets regulations provide for inspection
by hotels and other prescribed persons; they were not retrieved and may supply the
authority for some cases. Asserted.

### 3. The contact-address list excludes cousins, and a wrong one carries five years

s 10(2): a person who moves abroad "may" give a Singapore contact address, which
"must be" premises they still have an interest in, or the residence of a listed
relative: spouse, grandparent, parent, sibling, child, grandchild, the spouse's
grandparents, parents and siblings, aunts and uncles (the spouse's too), and nephews
and nieces (the spouse's too). **Cousins, step-relations, a sibling's spouse and
friends are not on it.** s 16(1)(c): giving a contact address "that is false or is
not an address described in section 10(2)(a) or (b)" is the five-year offence.
Giving a cousin's flat in good faith is an offence. Asserted across seventeen
relations and nine fixtures.

### 4. Giving your true foreign address on demand is grounds for arrest without warrant

s 22(1)(b)(iii): a person who, on demand by a registration officer or police
officer, "gives as his or her address a place outside Singapore" may be arrested
without warrant. Limb (iii) does not ask whether the address is false; limb (ii)
covers false ones. A Singaporean resident in London who truthfully answers with the
London address is within it. Asserted.

### 5. Cancellation for "mistake" does not ask whose mistake, and there is no appeal

s 13(1)(b): the Minister may order a registration cancelled if it "was effected by
mistake". The Registry's own mistake qualifies. s 13(2): the person "must
forthwith surrender" the card, and failing to is the five-year offence in
s 16(1)(g). The Act provides no appeal. Contrast s 21(3): refusing to surrender a
**suspicious** card on an officer's demand is only $5,000 or **six months**.
Asserted.

### 6. Preparing to commit an offence is the offence

s 19(1): a person who attempts **or "does any act preparatory to"** an offence
under the Act "shall be deemed to be guilty of the offence". Preparation carries the
full penalty, up to ten years for the s 16(2) offences. Asserted.

### 7. You may refuse an officer who does not identify themselves

s 26(1): an officer exercising a power must declare their office if not in uniform,
and produce identification on demand. s 26(2): "Despite any provision of this Act,
it is not an offence" to refuse a request, demand or order from an officer who has
not complied. A uniformed officer who refuses to show identification when asked
loses the power to make refusal an offence. Asserted.

### 8. An identity card does not prove itself

s 14: the onus of proving the contents of an application or an identity card true
is on the applicant, the card holder, "or on any other person alleging the truth of
the contents". s 15: a claim to citizenship by birth needs a birth certificate or
other evidence to the officer's satisfaction; an unnamed birth certificate, even
with a statutory declaration, or an old 1955 card stating birth in Singapore, "is
not necessarily sufficient". Asserted.

### 9. The receiving department is immune however the error was made

s 11(2)(a): the Government and the transmitting officers are immune for errors in
transmitted change-of-address information **if** the error was made in good faith
in the ordinary course of duty. s 11(2)(b): the department that receives it is
immune for errors "**however caused**" by the transmitting officer. Asserted.

### 10. Smaller things worth recording

- **s 2(3):** new personal identifiers must be images, measurements or recordings
  of an **external** body part or of the voice, and never an invasive sample.
  Fingerprints, a face photograph, an iris image and a signature are in the
  Schedule now.
- **s 3(5):** registration officers drawn from public authorities or prescribed
  institutions can register people but cannot demand surrender, arrest, search,
  investigate or serve court notices; only public officers can.
- **s 6:** registration is required of every person "lawfully resident", not only
  citizens and permanent residents; anyone else is excused only by regulations not
  retrieved here.
- **s 8:** a name must be in the modern English alphabet (or "permitted
  characters"); a patronymic like "bin" that does not match the registered sex may
  be refused.
- **s 17:** a registration officer may pass HDB's confidential data to the police
  or immigration only **with HDB's consent**, unless it is for criminal proceedings.
- **s 22(5):** a woman may be searched only by a woman.
- **s 25(5):** a no-show on a court notice may be arrested only if the court is
  satisfied of service, and not if the offender compounded or the notice was
  cancelled first.
- **s 27:** Malaysian identity-card and fingerprint officers' reports are
  admissible if the Public Prosecutor gives the accused a copy **ten clear days**
  before trial.
- **s 28:** composition is capped at **$500**, for offences carrying up to $10,000.
- **s 32:** registered post takes effect two days after posting even if returned;
  the Act states no time at all for personal delivery, leaving with an adult, or
  affixing a copy at the address.

## What would need doing before this is worth anything

- **Retrieve the National Registration Regulations.** Exemptions from registration,
  inspection by hotels, card categories and compoundable offences are all there,
  and they decide how far findings 2 and 3 bite in practice.
- **No case law was searched**, and no prosecution data. Findings 1 to 5 describe
  maximum penalties and powers as written, not how they are used.
- The cross-border railway extension (s 16A) and the procedure for arrested persons
  are not encoded.
- Whether an address is "true", a suspicion "reasonable" or an act "preparatory" is
  a supplied fact.
