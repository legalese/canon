## Why this Act, and why scoped

Chosen for its everyday-life relevance: anyone invited to join a sales network,
recruit friends or sign up under an upline meets it. It is a short Act, so the row
takes nearly all of the operative text: the definitions (s 2), the offence (s 3),
the registration offences (ss 4, 5), officers' liability (s 6) and the
benefit-stripping penalty (s 7). Not encoded: s 2(3), s 7(3) and (4), s 8 (District
Court jurisdiction), s 9 (Act prevails) and s 10 (regulations). Any order made under
s 2(2) excluding schemes was not retrieved.

## What the Act turns out to say

### 1. "Multi-level marketing" is defined as pyramid selling

s 2(1): "multi-level marketing scheme or arrangement" has the same meaning as
"pyramid selling scheme or arrangement". The Act draws no line between the two. A
scheme escapes only if it fails one of the three limbs of the definition or is
excluded by an order of the Minister under s 2(2). Whether any particular network
marketing business is lawful therefore turns on an order that is not in this
deposit. Asserted (with the order modelled as a flag).

### 2. The participant commits the same offence as the organiser

s 3(1) makes it unlawful "to promote or participate in" such a scheme, or to hold out
that one is doing so. The maximum is the same for all of them: a fine of $200,000 or
5 years' imprisonment or both (s 3(2)). The same maximum applies to applying for a
business name for such a business (s 4) and to a company incorporated for one, and
every officer of it (s 5). Asserted.

### 3. A scheme needs a benefit flowing up as well as down

The definition has three limbs, all needed: (a) a person can acquire a commodity (or
a right to one) for distribution; (b) that person benefits from recruiting, or from
the actions, performance or sales of additional participants; and (c) a benefit is
or may be received by some other promoter or participant. A referral bonus with
nobody above the referrer benefiting, or a single-level reseller, falls outside it.
"Commodity" covers services, rights and intangibles. Asserted.

### 4. The court must strip the benefit, but only for promoting or participating

s 7(1): on conviction for promoting or participating under s 3(2), where the person
received a benefit that is money or can be valued, the court "is ... to order" a
penalty up to the benefit's value, on top of any other punishment. The wording does
not reach a conviction for holding out, or under s 4 or s 5. For a participant the
court "may take into account" what was paid to join and any loss (s 7(2)); this is a
discretion, so only the ceiling is encoded. s 7(4) keeps any right to damages.
Asserted.

### 5. Directors and managers are deemed guilty, and must prove their way out

s 6(1) deems a director, general manager, manager, secretary or other officer
concerned in management, or anyone purporting to act as one, guilty of the company's
offence. The defence in s 6(2) needs both no consent or connivance and proper
diligence, and the individual must prove it. "Company" here includes a firm or other
association (s 6(3)). The encoding reads "concerned in the management" as qualifying
only "other officer"; that is an inference, and the other reading is open. Asserted.

### 6. What is not a "benefit"

A sample bought at or below cost and not for resale, and the time and effort spent
selling or recruiting, are not benefits (s 2(1)). Everything else is: commissions,
cross commissions, bonuses, refunds, discounts, dividends, services or "advantage of
whatever description". Asserted.
