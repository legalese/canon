## Why this Act, and why scoped

Chosen for its **everyday-life relevance**: anyone in Singapore who posts, forwards or
sells online services can meet it. (An automated count found 1 of the 527 deposited

## What the Act turns out to say

### 1. You can be ordered to correct a post without knowing it was false

ss 11(4) and 12(4): a Correction or Stop Communication Direction may be issued "even
if the person does not know or has no reason to believe that the statement is false".
s 10(2): it may be issued even after the statement was amended or taken down. The
only conditions are a false statement of fact communicated in Singapore and a
Minister's opinion that a Direction is in the public interest. Asserted.

### 2. The court cannot review the public-interest judgement

s 17(5): the High Court "may only set aside" a Part 3 Direction because the appellant
did not communicate the statement, it is not a statement of fact or is true, or
compliance is technically impossible. The Minister's public-interest opinion is not a
listed ground, and neither is the appellant's lack of knowledge. Before any appeal
the person must first have applied to the Minister and been refused (s 17(2)).
Asserted.

### 3. Appealing does not suspend the duty to comply

s 15(2)(b): it is "not a defence" to non-compliance that the person has applied to vary
or has appealed; nor is a contractual or professional duty (s 15(2)(a)). The Direction
"remains in effect despite the appeal" (s 17(6)); the only relief is a court stay on a
prima facie case of technical impossibility (s 17(7)). Non-compliance carries up to
$20,000 or 12 months for an individual. Asserted.

### 4. "Partly misleading" is false, and SMS counts only for the offences

s 2(2)(b): a statement is false if "false or misleading, whether wholly or in part, and
whether on its own or in the context in which it appears". s 3(2) extends
"communicated in Singapore" to SMS and MMS for Part 2 (the offences) only; elsewhere
(s 3(1)) it is the internet only, so on the text a Part 3 Direction does not reach an
SMS-only falsehood. That consequence is an inference from reading ss 3(1) and 10
together. Asserted.

### 5. The s 7 offence needs a listed harm; the bot offence does not

s 7(1) requires knowledge or reason to believe that the statement is false AND that it
is likely to cause one of six harms (security, public health or finances, foreign
relations, an election, inter-group enmity, confidence in Government). Penalty: $50,000
or 5 years for an individual, doubled to $100,000 or 10 years where a bot or
inauthentic account is used to accelerate it (s 7(3)). Making a bot intending it to
communicate a falsehood (s 8) is an offence with no harm element; a listed harm only
raises the maximum from $30,000/3 years to $60,000/6 years. Asserted.

### 6. Selling a falsehood service: offence only if used, and the fee is forfeited

s 9(1): taking a benefit for a service, knowing it will be used for falsehoods, is an
offence "if the service is in fact used". On conviction the court "must" order a
penalty equal to the benefit (s 9(5)), unless its value cannot be assessed (s 9(6)).
Asserted.

### 7. Three strikes in six months

s 32(1): an online location may be declared once 3 or more different statements under
active Directions are on it, at least 3 of them first posted within the 6 months
before. Spending money knowing it supports falsehoods on a declared location is an
offence (s 38: $40,000 or 3 years for an individual). Asserted.
