## Why this Act, and why scoped

This is requirement **REQ-0062**: Tier 2 of the remaining Singapore Acts, ordered by
everyday-life relevance. The requirement asks what the Act decides for a person or
business it applies to. No scenario has asked a sharper question yet.

The Act is short, so the row covers all of it except the short title: ss 2 to 5
(Part 1, the limit on liability) and ss 6, 7 (Part 2, the power of sale). Three things
are left out, because the Act assumes them and does not state them. One is the
innkeeper's common-law liability itself. Every amount the row computes assumes the
innkeeper would otherwise be liable for the whole loss, which is an inference. The
second is the "ordinary lien" that s 7 adds to. The third is what happens to a
shortfall when a sale does not cover the debt.

## What the Act turns out to say

### 1. A notice on the wall is worth the whole limit

Under s 5 an innkeeper has "the benefit of this Part" only for goods brought in
while a copy of s 3 is displayed. It must be "printed in plain type in English" in "a
conspicuous part of the hall or entrance". Without the notice, a guest whose $2,000
laptop is stolen from a room can recover all $2,000. With it, the guest gets $500.
Asserted.

### 2. The $500 limit does not cover cars or animals, yet the power of sale does cover cars

Under s 3(1) the limit applies to goods "not being a horse or other live animal, or any
gear appertaining thereto or any car or carriage". So the Act does not limit liability
for a car damaged in the hotel car park. Section 6 says that for Part 2 "goods"
includes any vehicle. Under s 7 the car can also be auctioned if it is left "in any
garage, car park or other premises appurtenant" while its owner owes for board or
lodging. Asserted.

### 3. Refusing safe custody can cost the innkeeper the limit, but only below $5,000

Under s 3(3) the innkeeper may refuse goods "the declared value of which exceeds
$5,000". Under s 4, refusing goods declared at $5,000 or less loses the benefit of
Part 1 for those goods. So does a guest being unable to deposit them "through any
default of the innkeeper or manager". A watch declared at $5,000, refused and then
stolen, is recoverable in full. A necklace declared at $8,000, refused and then stolen,
is capped at $500. Asserted.

### 4. Depositing goods lifts the $500 limit but caps recovery at the declared value

Exception (b) of s 3(1) covers goods "deposited expressly for safe custody". The
innkeeper "shall in no case be liable" above the declared value (s 3(3)). This row reads
"in no case" as applying even where staff were negligent, so jewellery declared at
$3,000 but worth $10,000 yields $3,000 either way. That reading is a fork. Where the
innkeeper asked for no declaration, the declared-value cap has nothing to bite on. The
row then allows the whole loss, which is an inference. A deposit that breaks a condition
the innkeeper required, such as an unsealed box, is treated as no deposit, so $500 applies.
That too is an inference from "as a condition of his liability". Asserted.

### 5. A restaurant gets the limit but not the power of sale

The Part 1 definition of "inn" in s 2 includes a "restaurant or other place of
refreshment", provided its keeper "is now by law responsible for the goods and property
of his guests". The Part 2 definition in s 6 is only "any hotel, tavern or public house".
A restaurant can cap a diner's claim at $500 but cannot auction a diner's bag. Asserted.

### 6. The sale is slow, public and limited to the lodging debt

Under s 7 the sale must be by public auction. It must come "after" 6 weeks unpaid, which
this row reads as more than 6 weeks. A notice must appear in a Singapore newspaper "at
least one month before". The sale may satisfy no "other or greater debt" than the lien
debt, and the innkeeper must pay the surplus "on demand". In the row, $5,000 proceeds
against a $1,200 lodging debt and $300 costs leave $3,500 for the guest. That holds even
if the guest owes the hotel $2,000 on something else. Asserted.

## What would need doing before this is worth anything

- The common law of innkeepers' liability and lien, which the Act limits and adds to,
  was not read. The amounts assume the innkeeper is otherwise fully liable.
- The forks in findings 4 and 6 need a lawyer or a decided case. These are "in no case"
  against negligence, the effect of a failed s 3(2) condition, and "after ... 6 weeks".
- No case law was searched. The question of which modern premises count as a "place of
  refreshment" whose keeper is "by law responsible" was not examined.
