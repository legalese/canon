## Why this Act, and why scoped

Chosen for its **everyday-life relevance** (Tier 1), not by citation count. Probation
and the absolute or conditional discharge are how many first and young offenders
avoid a sentence, and s 11 decides whether the conviction follows them afterwards.
This row takes ss 5 to 11. Appointments and committees (ss 3, 4), approved institutions
(s 12), rules (s 13), the copying duties, security for good behaviour (s 10(1)), the
enforcement mechanics of s 10(3) and the summons and warrant procedure of s 9 beyond
who has jurisdiction are not encoded. The maximum community service hours are in the
Criminal Procedure Code 2010 (s 5(7)), not in this Act, and are not encoded.

## What the Act turns out to say

### 1. An under-18 resentenced for the original offence still has no conviction

s 11(1) deems a conviction followed by probation or a discharge "not to be a
conviction" for other purposes. The proviso lifts that only where the offender was
"not less than 18 years of age at the time of his conviction" and is later sentenced
for the offence. An offender who was 17 at conviction and is later resentenced after a
breach keeps the protection; one who was 25 loses it. Asserted.

### 2. Disqualifications are disregarded "in any event"

s 11(2) separately requires the conviction to be disregarded for any written law that
imposes a disqualification or disability on convicted persons, "in any event", with no
proviso. Read literally, this survives even where s 11(1) has ceased to apply after a
resentence. That is an inference from the wording, not tested against case law.
Asserted on the literal reading.

### 3. For minimum-sentence offences, only 18 to 20 year-olds qualify, and only once

Probation and a discharge are never available for an offence whose sentence is
"fixed by law" (ss 5(1), 8(1)). Where a minimum or mandatory minimum sentence is
prescribed, the court may use them only if the offender "has attained 18 years of age
but has not attained 21" at conviction and has no previous conviction for such an
offence; s 11(1) does not wipe that previous conviction for this purpose. Read
literally, a 17-year-old fails the proviso. Whether such a case reaches this Act at all
depends on other Acts not read here. Asserted.

### 4. Consent is needed only from 18; supervisors of women must be women

s 5(9): the order must be explained "in ordinary language", and from 18 the offender
must express willingness to comply; under 18, no consent is required. s 6(3) applies
the same age line to amendments not sought by the probationer, except those only
cancelling or reducing a requirement. s 5(10): a woman or girl must be supervised by a
woman. Asserted.

### 5. The limits are tight and stated as numbers

Probation lasts 6 months to 3 years (s 5(1)); approved-institution residence at most 12
months (ss 5(4), 6(2)(b)), with a report to the court after 6 months (s 6(6)); a
conditional discharge at most 12 months (s 8(1)). An amendment cannot shorten the
period or carry it beyond 3 years from the original order (s 6(2)(a)). Breach can cost
a fine up to $1,000 or up to 14 days' detention while the order continues (s 7(2)).
Compensation is not a probation requirement (s 5(2) proviso) but a separate order,
capped at $500 in a Magistrate's Court (s 10(2)), and for an offender under 18 it is
enforced against the parent unless the parent cannot be found or did not contribute
by neglect (s 10(4)). Asserted.

### 6. Who deals with a breach or a further offence depends on which court made the order

A Magistrate's Court may resentence for a breach only if it made the order; otherwise
it commits or bails the probationer to the High Court or District Court that did
(s 7(2)). s 9(2) gives jurisdiction over a further offence to a High Court Judge, a
District Judge or a magistrate, a Youth Court order going to a magistrate. s 7(2) says
nothing of Youth Court orders; this row treats them as neither branch. Asserted.
