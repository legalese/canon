## Why this Act, and why scoped

This row answers **REQ-0050**: Tier 2 of the remaining Singapore Acts, ordered by
everyday-life relevance. The requirement asks what the Act decides for a person or
business it applies to; no scenario has asked a sharper question yet. The row takes
the conduct that makes someone a contemnor and the defences to it: scandalising
and prejudicing pending proceedings (s 3(1)(a), (b)) with the Part 4 defences
(ss 14, 16 to 20), abusive litigation (s 3(1)(da), (db)), disobedience and waiver
(ss 4, 21), recordings (ss 5, 21A), online publication (s 11(2)), maximum
punishment (s 12(1)), the non-publication offence (s 13(5)), the
Attorney-General's consent (s 30) and the start date (s 35(1)). Not encoded:
corporate and partnership attribution (ss 6, 7), common law and inherent powers
(ss 8, 9), each court's jurisdiction (s 10), the non-publication direction
procedure, police investigation and the Schedule (ss 22 to 24), procedure, video
links, bail, appeals and fine enforcement (ss 25 to 27, 31 to 33), third parties
who cause abusive litigation (s 3(7)) and postponement orders (s 14(4)).

## What the Act turns out to say

### 1. Permission to record a hearing is not permission to publish it

s 5(1)(a) and (aa) make bringing a recorder or recording "without the permission of
the court" contempt. But (b), publishing or transmitting a recording, carries no
permission proviso at all. The only way out is s 5(4): recordings made or used for
"official transcripts" or "any other purpose authorised by the court". So, on this
reading, a person allowed to record who then posts the recording commits contempt
unless the court authorised that use. s 21A (the online-hearing defence for those
who could not have known they were recording) covers (aa), (b) and (c), not
bringing a device into court under (a). Asserted.

### 2. Suing once on a groundless claim is not contempt; suing repeatedly is

The 2025 limbs (Act 45 of 2024, wef 28 January 2025) catch a proceeding that is a
deception or sham, or one that is "manifestly groundless" **and** brought for an
ulterior or improper purpose (s 3(1)(da)). For "multiple or successive" groundless
proceedings, no improper purpose is needed (s 3(1)(db)). A single groundless claim
brought for a proper purpose is neither. In each case knowing "or ought to know" is
enough. Asserted.

### 3. The Government's public-interest exemption covers prejudice, not scandal

s 3(4) exempts a Government statement about a pending case that the Government
"believes ... is necessary in the public interest", but only from s 3(1)(b). A
Government statement that impugns a court's integrity gets no exemption under
s 3(1)(a). Asserted.

### 4. Intent to publish is enough; defences are limb-specific and the burden is on you

s 3(2) and (3): the publisher is guilty even without intending to scandalise or to
prejudice. Each Part 4 defence names the limbs it reaches: a good-faith complaint to
the police or Chief Justice (s 16) and a good-faith court filing (s 17) answer only
scandalising. Not knowing the case was pending (s 20) answers only prejudice. A fair
and accurate report (s 14), innocent publication (s 18) and publication abroad
(s 19) answer both. s 29 puts the burden of proving any of them on the person
relying on it, on the balance of probabilities, while contempt itself must be proved
beyond reasonable doubt (s 28). Ss 14, 16 and 20 are asserted; ss 17, 18 and 19 are
encoded but not tested.

### 5. A party can forgive a breached order, but not a refusal to answer the court

s 4(4): breach of an order or undertaking, or disposal of attached property, "may be
waived by the aggrieved party", and the waiver relieves liability. The court may
disallow it on any of four grounds (s 4(5)). s 4(7): refusals to produce documents,
take the oath, answer or sign "may not be waived". The "aggrieved party" is defined
only for proceedings other than criminal ones (s 4(9)), so, as a reading, a breach in
criminal proceedings cannot be waived. s 21 excuses an honest and reasonable
misunderstanding of the obligation. Asserted.

### 6. The ceiling is $100,000 and 3 years in the superior courts, $20,000 and 12 months elsewhere

s 12(1): the General Division, the Appellate Division and the Court of Appeal may
fine up to $100,000 or imprison up to 3 years, or both. Every other court, and the
General Division when it deals with contempt of a State, Family or Youth Court, is
limited to $20,000 or 12 months. Ignoring an Attorney-General's non-publication
direction without reasonable excuse is a separate offence with the same lower
ceiling (s 13(5)). Online material "accessed by members of the public in Singapore"
is taken to be published here (s 11(2)). Asserted.

### 7. The Attorney-General's consent is needed for publication contempt, not for enforcing your own order

s 30(1): proceedings for contempt under s 3, or by a non-party abetting a breach
(s 4(8)), need the Attorney-General's consent. Contempt in the face of the court and
proceedings a superior court starts on its own motion do not (s 30(2)). A party
enforcing an order against the other party under s 4(1) is not named, so (an
inference) needs no consent. The Act does not apply to contempt committed before
1 October 2017 (s 35(1)). Asserted.

## What would need doing before this is worth anything

- Corporate officers' liability (ss 6, 7) and the s 3(7) third-party abuse limbs
  are not encoded.
- The Rules of Court and Family Justice Rules governing procedure (s 26) were not
  retrieved.
- Whether the 2025 abuse limbs apply to proceedings begun before 28 January 2025 is
  not settled by the text.
