## Why this Act, and why scoped

**5 of the 527 Singapore Acts** deposited here cite it. It is a grab-bag of
summary offences, so this row takes the ones with decision content an ordinary
person or business meets: the dog owner's liability (s 10), spitting (s 11(1)(f)),
calls to emergency numbers and the subscriber's liability (ss 14A, 14B), false
messages (s 14D), soliciting and disorderly behaviour (ss 19, 20), trespass
(s 21), touting (ss 32, 33), fraudulent possession and the secondhand dealer's
duty (ss 35, 36), melting precious metals (ss 37, 39) and lending out a SIM card
(s 39B). Not encoded: the road, animal and canal offences in ss 6-9, 12, 13, 16;
noise (ss 14, 15); vagrancy (Part 4); ss 14C, 14E, 39C-39I; police powers;
corporate liability (ss 40A, 40B); and the Schedule of scam offences.

## What the Act turns out to say

### 1. Lending your SIM card can now attract caning

s 39B(1) makes it an offence to provide a SIM card registered in one's own name
knowing or having reasonable grounds to believe it is for an offence. Under
s 39B(2), those grounds are presumed if the act is done "for any gain" or without
reasonable steps to ascertain the recipient's identity and location, or the
purpose. s 39B(5), added by Act 21 of 2025, makes an individual also liable to up
to 12 strokes if he or she knew it was for a scam, or if the card was proved used
for a scam and the individual cannot prove reasonable steps against that. An
individual faces $10,000 or 3 years; any other offender a $20,000 fine only.
Asserted.

### 2. A dog owner is liable without proof of fault, but not to intruders

s 10(4): no need to show "a previous vicious propensity", the owner's knowledge, or
neglect. Compensation of up to $2,000 is payable on top of a fine of up to $5,000.
But an injury inside a house or premises creates neither criminal liability nor
compensation unless the person entered on duty or with permission (s 10(7), (8)),
and an injury caused by the victim's own wrongful act creates none (s 10(9)). The
occupier where the dog was kept is deemed the owner unless he or she proves both
non-ownership and lack of sanction or knowledge (s 10(5)). Asserted.

### 3. Touting carries a minimum fine

s 32 sets a fine "of not less than $1,000 and not more than $5,000" (second or
subsequent: $2,000 to $10,000), one of the few minimum fines in the Act. Touting
at a public office (s 33) does not catch an officer appointed to advise the public
or a licensed petition writer. Asserted.

### 4. Melting stolen gold within 5 days is an offence only if it really was stolen

s 37 (Act 21 of 2025) catches a pawnbroker, secondhand dealer, or worker or dealer
in platinum, gold or silver who melts or sells goods within 5 days of receipt
from a non-dealer, or after police notice, without CID permission, but only if
the goods "were in fact stolen or fraudulently obtained". s 39 excludes their
employees. Reading "within 5 days" as 5 days or fewer is an inference; the cases
avoid the boundary. Asserted.

### 5. A false message is excused only by a legitimate purpose the accused proves

s 14D(1A), added by Act 16 of 2024, is illustrated by a government
simulated-phishing email. The burden is on the accused, on a balance of
probabilities. Asserted.

### 6. Repeat offences raise the ceiling

Spitting rises from $1,000 to $2,000 on a repeat conviction (s 11(2)). Soliciting
carries no imprisonment on a first conviction but up to 6 months on a later one
(s 19). Disorderly behaviour rises to $5,000 or 12 months (s 20). Fraudulent
possession rises from $3,000 to $5,000 after a previous conviction, including one
in Malaysia or Brunei Darussalam (s 35(5)). A call to an emergency number from a
public telephone doubles the fine (s 14A(3)). Asserted.

### 7. The subscriber answers for the line

s 14B: the subscriber to a telephone service used for a s 14A call is guilty
unless he or she proves due diligence. This does not apply to a public telephone
service. A caller who stays silent or hangs up is presumed to intend to annoy
(s 14A(4)(b)). Asserted.
