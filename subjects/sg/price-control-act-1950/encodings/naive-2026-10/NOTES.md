## Why this Act, and why scoped

Chosen for its **everyday-life relevance**: it is the Act under which the state can
fix a maximum price for goods or services, and it puts duties on every shop dealing
in such goods. This row takes the offences a seller or shopper meets: selling and
buying above the maximum (s 9, with the saving in s 14), displaying prices (ss 7, 8),
refusing to sell (s 10), and the penalty provisions (s 16(1)-(4), (6)). Appointments,
the content of price and controlled-article orders (ss 4, 5), parliamentary control,
inspection, arrest and seizure, obstruction, attempts and abetment, evidence, venue,
delegation, rules and forms are not encoded. No price order was retrieved, so whether
any goods are price-regulated today is not known from the deposit.

## What the Act turns out to say

### 1. The buyer commits an offence too

s 9(2): anyone who "knowingly purchases or offers to purchase" price-regulated goods,
or knowingly pays for a service, above the fixed maximum is guilty of an offence.
Knowledge is required for the buyer but not, on the words of s 9(1), for the seller.
The only shelter is s 14: no proceedings against a person "duly authorised" by the
Price Controller who buys "with the intention of procuring evidence" against the
seller. Both conditions are needed. Asserted.

### 2. "Sell" includes putting a price tag on the shelf, and the price is net of discounts

s 2 defines "sell" to include an offer, and an offer to include "the exposing of goods
for sale, the publication of a price list, the furnishing of a quotation". So an
over-priced label is itself an offence under s 9(1), with no sale needed. The
"selling price" is the net price "less all discounts or other allowances", so a list
price above the maximum, discounted below it, is not over the limit. Asserted
(at the maximum exactly is not "exceeds").

### 3. A shop with stock cannot refuse to sell, unless it doubts cash payment

s 10: a business that normally sells price-regulated goods and holds stock commits an
offence if it falsely denies having them, or refuses to sell "in reasonable
quantities" without the Price Controller's permission. The proviso makes it a defence
that the seller reasonably believed the buyer could not or would not pay "immediate
payment ... in cash". The defence is to paragraph (b) only; it does not cover a false
denial. Asserted.

### 4. Directors are presumed guilty with their company, and the default penalties are modest

s 16(4): when a body corporate is convicted, every director or officer at the time "shall
be deemed to be guilty" unless he proves lack of knowledge or all due diligence. The
default maxima (s 16(1), (2)) are $2,000 or 2 years (individual) and $10,000 (body
corporate) for a first offence, rising to $20,000 or 5 years and $20,000 for a repeat.
A company's first-offence cap is five times an individual's, but both repeat caps are
$20,000. These apply "where no other penalty is specifically provided"; orders and
rules may set their own, capped by s 16. Asserted.

### 5. A court can shut a trader down

s 16(3): on convicting a trader or commission agent, the court may debar him, his firm
or a corporation of which he is an officer "from carrying on business" for any period
it sets. Breaching the order: $20,000 or one year. s 16(6) makes a principal liable for
a partner, agent or servant unless he proves "all reasonable precautions". Asserted.

### 6. The deposit's arrangement of sections is one number out

The ARRANGEMENT OF SECTIONS lists a blank entry for 1 and "Short title" against 2, so
every heading is one number high. The body, and the Act's own cross-references (s 2
refers to maximum prices "fixed under section 4" and controlled articles under
"section 5(a)"), use the body numbering, which this row follows. Not asserted (an
observation about the deposit).
