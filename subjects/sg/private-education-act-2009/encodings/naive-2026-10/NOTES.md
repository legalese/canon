## Why this Act, and why scoped

Chosen for its **everyday-life relevance** (Tier 1 of the remaining Acts), not by
how often other Acts cite it. Anyone in Singapore who pays a private diploma
provider, an international school or a full-time exam-preparation course deals with
an institution this Act regulates. When a course fails to start or a school closes,
the refund and notice rules are what the student meets.

This row takes what counts as private education (the Schedule), who is outside the
Act (s 45), the duty to register and the bar on suing for fees (s 7), two grounds
for refusing registration (s 10(1)(a), (c)), the 6-month suspension cap and 14-day
wait (s 11(1), (5)), notice of closure (s 14(1)(b)), course money refunds (s 33),
misleading advertisements (s 19), and the penalty ceilings (ss 21, 34, 40). Not
encoded: the Agency (Part 2), registration procedure, the other refusal and
suspension grounds, names, course permission beyond its effect on s 7(10), teachers,
accreditation schemes, appeals, inspection, requisitions, registers, dispute
resolution schemes and the transitional provisions.

## What the Act turns out to say

### 1. A weekend tuition class is not "private education"; a part-time diploma is

The Schedule lists five kinds of private education. Only (a), "education leading to
the award of a diploma or degree", is caught whatever the hours. The other four
(certificates, foreign-curriculum schooling, special education, exam preparation)
count only if **full-time**: at least one month, 5 days a week, at least 3 hours a
day. So a part-time evening diploma is private education, but a part-time
certificate course, a weekend tuition class and a two-week exam boot camp are not.
Reading "5 days of a week" as "at least 5" is an inference. Asserted.

### 2. An unregistered or unpermitted provider cannot sue for its fees

s 7(10): no one may "recover in any court" course money for private education
provided in Singapore unless the provider is registered **and** gave the course
under the Agency's s 16 permission. A registered provider running a course without
permission is also barred. Asserted.

### 3. A refund under s 33 is the Agency's power, not the student's automatic right

s 33 applies only when the course does not start on the agreed day, stops part-way,
or is cut short by regulatory action, and only if the student has not withdrawn
before that "default day". The Agency then "may" direct a refund of the whole or an
"equitable" part. Once a refund is directed, the student can recover it as a civil
debt "despite anything contained in the agreement or contract". The duty ends if the
student accepts a place elsewhere **at the institution's expense** (s 33(4)). The
Act does not list a change of mind or poor teaching as a trigger. Asserted.

### 4. A school that closes by choice owes more notice than one shut down

s 14(1)(b): students must be told in writing, with arrangements for a place
elsewhere, at least **14 days** ahead when the closure follows non-renewal,
suspension or cancellation, but at least **30 days** ahead "for any other reason",
including the owners' own decision. Asserted.

### 5. Only companies and registered societies are safe from refusal

s 10(1)(a): the Agency may refuse registration if the institution "is not a company
or a registered society". A sole proprietor or partnership is within the Act (the
s 2 definition of "manager" provides for both) but may be refused for its form. The
teachers ground (s 10(1)(c)) needs "all or more than half" of them to be unqualified,
so exactly half is not enough. Asserted.

### 6. Most offences fall to the $10,000 default, with a daily fine

Running an unregistered institution, ignoring a refund direction and a manager's
failure to give closure notice have no penalty of their own. They take the s 34(2)
default: $10,000 or 12 months or both, plus up to $1,000 a day after conviction. A
misleading advertisement (s 19(3)) and the s 34(1) offences are capped at $5,000 or 6
months. Composition is capped at the lower of half the maximum fine or $5,000
(s 40). The s 19 presumption puts the burden on the advertiser: an advertisement that
falsely describes the institution or omits prescribed information is "presumed,
unless the contrary is proved", to be misleading. Asserted.

### 7. Housekeeping in the deposit

In the extracted text, the arrangement of sections at the top reads one number out
of step with the body from the dispute resolution section (s 37 in the body) onwards;
this may be a layout artefact of extraction. The body's own cross-references (s 6 to
ss 21, 37 and 40; s 10 to s 44) match the body's numbering, and this row follows it. s 46(2) and (4) still
refer to "the Council", which no longer appears anywhere else in the Act. Not asserted.

## What would need doing before this is worth anything

- The Private Education Regulations (prescribed courses, teacher criteria, dispute
  resolution schemes, advertisement content) were not retrieved, and many of the
  rules turn on them.
- No Ministerial exemption orders (s 41) or Gazette exclusions (s 2) were checked.
- The new "private education institution for adult learners" definition (Act 17 of
  2026) is used in this deposit only in s 4(2)(e) and was not encoded.
- The 14-day counting in s 11(5) and the "5 days of a week" reading are assumptions.
