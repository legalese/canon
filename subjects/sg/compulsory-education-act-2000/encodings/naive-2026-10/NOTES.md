## Why this Act, and why scoped

Chosen for its **everyday-life relevance**: it is the Act that makes primary school
compulsory for Singapore citizen children and makes their parents criminally liable
when a child does not attend. It is short (12 sections). This row takes who must
attend (s 2, s 3(1)), the parent's offence and its exceptions (s 3(2), (3), s 4), the
Board's quorum (s 5(8)), the offences against the Board and the penalties (s 7),
prosecution only on the Director-General's complaint (s 8) and composition (s 9).
The rest of the Board's constitution, its duties and powers as such (s 6), the
protection from liability (s 10), the saving of the Education Act 1957 (s 11) and
regulations (s 12) are not encoded. The Director-General's conditions, the
prescribed reasons for absence, any prescribed schools, exemption orders and the
list of compoundable offences were not retrieved; they are inputs.

## What the Act turns out to say

### 1. Every parent is separately guilty, and "parent" reaches past the parents

s 3(2): if the child fails to attend regularly, "each parent of the child shall be
guilty of an offence". s 2: "parent" "includes a guardian and any person having the
actual custody of the child". So a mother, a father, a guardian and a custodian may
each commit the offence on the same facts. Reading mother and father in, and leaving
out a relative without guardianship or custody, are inferences from the word
"includes". Asserted.

### 2. Only citizens are covered

s 3(1)(b), (c): the duty falls on a child who is "a citizen of Singapore" and
"residing in Singapore". A permanent-resident or foreign child, or a citizen child
living abroad, is outside s 3 altogether. Asserted.

### 3. The parent's offence and lying to the Board carry the same maximum

s 7(1) and (2) each give "a fine not exceeding $5,000 or ... imprisonment for a term
not exceeding 12 months or ... both", whether the parent failed to send the child to
school or knowingly gave the Board false information. Composition, where the offence is
prescribed as compoundable, is capped at $2,000 (s 9), and no one may be charged at all
except on the Director-General's complaint (s 8). Asserted.

### 4. The Board can order counselling, but refusing it is not listed as an offence

s 6(2)(d) lets the Board "require any person to attend any counselling or mediation
session". s 7(2) makes it an offence to refuse a hearing, refuse information, knowingly
give false information, or obstruct or impede the Board; refusing counselling is not on
the list. Whether a refusal could amount to obstruction is not settled by the text;
treating it as no offence is an inference. Asserted.

### 5. "Compulsory school age" runs to 15, but the duty is only to primary school

s 2: a child of compulsory school age is "above the age of 6 years" and has "not yet
attained the age of 15 years", and meets the Director-General's conditions. Primary
education is "a 6-year course". The Act says nothing about secondary school. Reading
"above the age of 6" as strictly more than 6, so that a child of exactly 6 is outside,
is an inference. Asserted.

### 6. The 1996 birth cut-off excludes a child born on 1 January 1996

s 3(1)(a): "born after 1 January 1996". A child born that day was not covered; one born
the next day was. Any child under 15 today satisfies the condition, so it now matters
only historically. Asserted.

### 7. The Board's quorum is one-half

s 5(8): one-half of the members. A meeting of 5 out of 10 is quorate; 3 out of 7 is not.
Asserted.
