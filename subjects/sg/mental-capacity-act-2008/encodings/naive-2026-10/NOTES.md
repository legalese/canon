## Why this Act, and why scoped

**34 of the 527 Singapore Acts** deposited here cite it (s 4 by 9). It is the Act a
family meets when a parent develops dementia: who decides, on what test, under a
lasting power of attorney or a court deputy, and what no one may decide at all.
The Public Guardian, the court's procedure and the electronic system are not
encoded.

## What the Act turns out to say

### 1. No LPA donee may ever decide on life-sustaining treatment

s 13(8): "**Despite anything in the lasting power of attorney**, a donee authorised
to make decisions about P's personal welfare may not make any decision with respect
to ... life-sustaining treatment on P" or treatment needed to prevent a serious
deterioration. There is no override: an LPA that expressly confers that authority
confers none. Court deputies are barred the same way (s 25(3)(g)). Asserted.

### 2. A bank can refuse a donee unless the incapacity is "likely to be permanent"

s 13(10): a person dealing with a donee "may require the donee to produce a
certificate ... stating that P's lack of capacity is **likely to be permanent**",
and may refuse the donee's authority without it. But s 4(2) says incapacity counts
"whether ... permanent or temporary", and s 13(1) lets the donee act whenever P
lacks capacity. For a donor expected to recover -- after a stroke, say -- a third
party may lawfully refuse the donee who is lawfully entitled to act. Asserted.

### 3. A child who is a professional cannot be the parent's paid donee

s 12(1)(a)(ii), (b)(ii): a paid professional donee must be "**not related to P by
blood or marriage**". A family member may act only unpaid, as an individual aged 21
or over. Asserted.

### 4. An LPA with a gifts clause keeps a power no court deputy can ever have

s 14: a donee may make gifts from the donor's estate "if, and only if, the lasting
power of attorney contains express authorisation", and then only reasonable gifts
having regard to the estate. s 25(3)(a): a deputy "may not be given powers with
respect to ... the disposition of P's property by making gifts" at all. Asserted.

### 5. Property decisions must aim to preserve the estate for the person's own care

s 6(7): on any disposition of the person's property, the decider "must be motivated
by a desire to ensure ... that the person's property is preserved for application
towards the costs of the person's maintenance during the person's life". This is a
Singapore addition with no counterpart in the English Act. A sale of the home to
fund a child's flat fails it. Asserted.

### 6. The ill-treatment offence starts at 16; the rest of the Act at 21

s 4(5): no power under the Act may be exercised in relation to a person below **21**,
subject to s 21; and s 11(2)(c), s 12(1) require donors and individual donees to be
21. s 42: the ill-treatment offence protects a person who lacks capacity and is
"**16** years of age or above" -- up to 8 years, or 14 if death results. A 17-year-old
is protected by s 42 but cannot have an LPA donee. Asserted.

### 7. Joint donees by default, and one bad apple sinks the LPA

s 12(5): unless the instrument says otherwise, donees act **jointly**. s 12(6): if they
act jointly and one fails to qualify, "a lasting power of attorney" is not created at
all. Only a joint-and-several appointment saves the rest (s 12(7)). Asserted.

### 8. Smaller things worth recording

- **s 5:** the four abilities are alternatives; retaining information "for a short
  period only" is enough, and understanding with simple explanation counts.
- **s 8:** restraint -- including restricting movement when P does not resist -- is
  protected only if necessary to prevent harm to P and proportionate.
- **s 26, 28:** no one may decide for another on marriage, sexual touching, divorce,
  adoption, religion, gender reassignment, sterilisation, termination of pregnancy,
  organ objections, advance medical directives, body gifts, or voting.
- **s 13(9), (9A):** a donee can never make a will, an insurance nomination or a CPF
  nomination for the donor.
- **s 15(5)(c), (8):** divorce from the donor ends a spouse-donee's appointment unless
  the instrument says otherwise; a donee's bankruptcy ends only the property part.
