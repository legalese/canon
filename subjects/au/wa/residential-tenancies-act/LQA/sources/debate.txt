Bills

Residential Tenancies Amendment (Rent Cap) Bill 2026

Introduction and first reading

Bill introduced, on motion by Hon Tim Clifford, and read a first time.

Second reading speech

Hon Tim Clifford (10:15 am): I move:

That the bill be now read a second time.

This bill addresses the central social justice issue in the lives of many Western
Australians--the affordability and security of housing in the rental market. Housing is
not simply another sector of the economy. It is a human right. It also underpins social
stability, economic participation and individual wellbeing. The ability to maintain a stable
home affects employment, education, health outcomes and community connection.
Basically, it affects everything.

We are living through a housing crisis that is not abstract, not theoretical and not
temporary. It is showing up in families cutting back on essentials just to pay rent; people
living week to week, one rent increase away from eviction; workers sleeping in cars;
young people locked out of independence; and older renters facing the real prospect of
homelessness. Let us be honest with ourselves; this is not just a supply story, because
we cannot supply our way out of what is fundamentally a wealth inequality crisis. Yes,
we need more housing, especially public and affordable housing, and the Greens have
been absolutely consistent on that, but lack of supply alone does not explain why rents
can jump by hundreds of dollars in a year. It certainly does not explain why, in a wealthy
state like Western Australia, housing insecurity has become so normalised. We are not
just dealing with a shortage. We are dealing with a system that is tilted--tilted towards
speculation and treating housing primarily as an investment vehicle and tilted away from
the people who live in these homes.

I acknowledge the announcements from the government over the last week or so. The
end of no-fault evictions, assuming that a ban is implemented in full, is an incredibly

welcome development that will go a long way to providing some security for renters.
However, the government must do so much more to address the structural problems
causing so much distress to renters in this state. The government needs to take further
action right now. We cannot always be last to the table on these issues. When we lag
behind the rest of the country--we have seen it happen time and again--real people
suffer dire consequences.

Our bill introduces a crucial reform designed to address a structural issue in a measured
and targeted way. It does so through two principal mechanisms. First, it introduces a
framework to moderate rent increases during a tenancy and, second, it strengthens
security of tenure through changes to termination provisions. This bill is about
something fundamental--whether people in Western Australia are allowed to have a
home they can rely on, not just a roof over their heads for now and not just somewhere
they can stay until the next rent increase notice arrives, but a home that is stable,
secure and affordable enough that they can plan their lives with some degree of
certainty. Right now, for far too many Western Australians, that is simply not the reality.

This bill takes two clear practical steps to rebalance the system. First, it puts a flexible
indexed limit on rent increases. Second, it ends no-cause evictions. I know the
government has indicated that it will deal with this issue, but we do not know exactly
what approach will be taken. I could not put forward a rent cap bill that did not deal with
no-fault evictions, because no-fault evictions make a rent cap unworkable. Our bill is a
complete package that is designed to deal with the structural issue of market-based
rental insecurity.

These are not radical ideas in the sense of untried or disruptive measures. We are
talking about provisions that have existed in other jurisdictions for years. They are basic
protections that recognise a simple truth: if everything is left to "the market" in a housing
crisis, the outcome is not efficiency; it is displacement. Under the existing framework,
rent increases are subject to certain requirements but there is no general statutory limit
on the amount by which rent can be increased during a tenancy. In a constrained rental
market, this results in increases that are impossible for tenants to absorb within short
timeframes.

Our bill will introduce a new provision, section 31AA, which limits the amount by which
rent may be increased during a tenancy. The limit is determined by a formula based on
movements in the consumer price index for Perth, specifically the rents subgroup, with a
multiplier applied--basically a system that is aligned with the Australian Capital
Territory. Members should note that if we measure the growth of median rents versus
wage growth from financial years 2021­22 to 2024­25, the rent-to-wage ratio in
Canberra was 1.1. In Perth, it was 5.2. That is the difference between a difficult rental
market and a catastrophe.

The indexed rent cap provides a structured and transparent method for adjusting rents
over time. By linking permissible increases to CPI, the bill allows rents to respond to
general economic conditions, including inflationary pressures affecting lessors. At the
same time, it seeks to reduce the incidence of sharp or unpredictable increases that put
tenants under financial strain. It is important to emphasise that this mechanism does not
prevent rent increases. It regulates the extent of those increases within a defined
framework.

The bill also makes provision for circumstances in which a higher increase may be
appropriate. New section 31AB will allow a lessor to apply to a competent court for
approval to increase rent above the prescribed limit. In determining such an application,
the court is required to consider a range of factors, including comparable rents, the
capital value of the premises, the outgoings borne by the lessor, and the condition and
amenities of the property. This ensures that the framework retains a degree of flexibility
and responsiveness. It recognises that individual properties and circumstances may
differ, and that a uniform limit may not be appropriate in all cases. The bill provides a
mechanism for independent assessment and oversight.

Under this bill, the amount of increase is tied to CPI, with a modest margin. That is it. It
does not freeze rents, although a rent freeze will become necessary if measures like
this are not adopted soon. It does not impose arbitrary limits disconnected from
economic reality. We only have to look to the ACT to see a rent cap mechanism at
work. Our bill simply says that increases should broadly track inflation, not exceed it by
large and unpredictable margins.

I want to be very clear about this next point, because it will be contested. This is not
about punishing landlords, though it will affect investment decisions. It is about
recognising that housing is not just another commodity. When the price of luxury goods
goes up, people can choose not to buy them. When rent goes up, people do not have
that choice. They cut back on food. They skip medical care. They take on debt. That is
the difference. That is why it is entirely reasonable--at this point, necessary--for
Parliament to set limits.

Inevitably, there will be claims that this is too rigid, that it fails to account for individual
circumstances and it will discourage investment. This is where it is important to look at
what the bill does. The bill is not rigid; it explicitly allows for flexibility. If a lessor believes
that a higher increase is justified because of increased costs, improvements to the
property, or market conditions, they can apply to have a higher increase approved.
But--this is the crucial point--that decision will be made transparently, based on
evidence and with regard to fairness. It is not made unilaterally or behind closed doors.
It is not imposed on a tenant with no recourse and not just because the Real Estate
Institute of Western Australia or a real estate agent says it is because of the market.

That is what our bill introduces--accountability. With no-fault evictions eliminated, an
indexed rent cap can stabilise rents. The bill clearly sets out a list of genuine reasons
why a lessor might have to end a tenancy: moving into the property; housing a family
member; selling the property; undertaking major works; or changing the use of the
premises. These are reasonable grounds. They reflect legitimate circumstances in
which a tenancy might need to end. They do not include "because I can get more rent
from someone else". That is the point.

Our bill also introduces requirements relating to the content of rent increase notices. A
notice must state the amount of the proposed increase, whether it exceeds the
prescribed limit, and, if so, the requirement for tenant agreement or court approval.
These provisions are intended to promote transparency and to ensure that tenants are
clearly informed of their rights and options. Clear communication is an important
element of a fair tenancy framework.

Our bill will amend section 70A in the act to provide that a residential tenancy
agreement does not automatically terminate at the end of a fixed term unless the tenant
gives notice. The bill also recognises that social housing providers operate in a different
context, but it still requires that terminations occur on specified grounds and with
appropriate notice. It also provides tenants with the ability to seek relief from a court.
Again, this is about balance.

Finally, the bill includes transitional provisions to ensure that nobody is disadvantaged
by timing. In particular, certain termination notices given before commencement will
cease to have effect. This ensures that the new protections are meaningful from the
outset. All of this matters because large, arbitrary rent increases destroy renters' sense
of security. Security of tenure is not an abstract concept. It is about whether people can
keep their kids in the same school, stay connected to their community, maintain
employment without disruption, and look after their health and wellbeing. We know now
that rent increases are driving people from house to house. When people are forced to
move repeatedly, everything falls apart. We know this.

My office has conducted a survey of renters to get an idea of what is going on, out in the
real world. I have to say, some of the stories are heartbreaking and some of the
numbers are eye-watering. Rent increases of 50% over two or three years are not
unheard of and increases between 30% and 40% are becoming common. People
cannot cope with this; wages do not increase at that rate. If members do not believe in
figures generated by my office, I urge them to read Anglicare's Rental Affordability
Snapshot: National Report 2026. The situation is dire. I seek leave to table the report.

Leave granted.

Hon Tim Clifford: We will hear the usual arguments. We will hear that a rent cap will
distort the market. We will hear that investors will leave. We will hear that supply will dry
up. These claims are made every time tenant protections are proposed and they are
always overstated. What this bill does is modest but it will address a structural issue
directly. It will allow rent increases in line with inflation. It will allow higher increases with
court approval. It will allow termination on genuine grounds. What it will not allow is
excessive rent increases disconnected from costs. If a system depends on those
practices to function, then the problem is not government intervention or reform. The
problem is the system.

At its core, this bill reflects a back-to-basics way of thinking about housing--not as a
speculative asset first and a home second, but as a human need, something that should
be stable. Something that should be predictable. Something that should not be a source
of constant anxiety. That does not mean ignoring the role of private investment, but it
does mean setting clear and reasonable boundaries.

Obviously, this bill is not the whole solution to the housing crisis. Our community needs
much more, such as: a significant investment in public housing; stronger planning for
affordable housing; action on vacancy, short-stays and land banking; and above all, a
long-term back-to-basics shift in how we think about housing policy. The ideas in this bill
are a necessary part of the response because they deal with what is happening right
now. It is time to give tenants some breathing room, introduce some fairness, and
restore some balance. At the end of the day, we are faced with a simple question:
Should people be able to stay in their homes without facing unpredictable rent hikes and
eviction through financial pressure? If the answer to that question is yes--it should be--
then this bill deserves support. It is measured. It is practical and it responds directly to
the reality that people across Western Australia are facing.

Pursuant to standing order 126(1), I advise that this bill is not a uniform legislation bill. It
does not ratify or give effect to an intergovernmental or multilateral agreement to which
the government of the state is a party; nor does this bill, by reason of its subject matter,
introduce a uniform scheme or uniform laws throughout the Commonwealth.

I commend the bill to the house and I table the explanatory memorandum.
